How the games were made

Three things can go wrong with a book of chess games. The moves can be wrong. The attribution can be wrong, so that an assessment gets put in the wrong person’s mouth. And the story can be wrong, repeated out of an anthology because it is too good to check. This chapter is about what is done here to catch the first, and what is admitted about the other two.

Where the moves come from

Every game was transcribed from a published record. For most of them that record is the game’s page at chessgames.com, which is also the source the Wikipedia article on each game cites; where a game has a Wikipedia article of its own, the move list was taken from there and compared against chessgames.com. The sources are listed with each game and collected in Sources.

Nothing here is reconstructed, and nothing is supplied from memory. Where two sources disagree, both are listed and the chapter says so.

Whether the game was played

A game being in a book of famous games is not evidence that it happened. Four here carry a doubt, and each says so in its own chapter:

  • Greco, 1623. Probably composed rather than played. His manuscripts never name an opponent, and Hooper and Whyld hold the games in them to be quite possibly constructs: instructive examples rather than scores. It is the oldest thing in the book, and it is here as what it is.
  • Légal’s Mate, c. 1750. The move order appears to have been tidied up afterwards to remove a flaw, and the traditional date is probably wrong, with 1787 more likely than 1750.
  • The Immortal Game, 1851. Kieseritzky is thought to have resigned at about move twenty, so the mate the game is famous for may never have been played on a board.
  • The Evergreen Game, 1852. The first publication of it said only that White mates in four, so the finish may have been announced rather than played.

The other thirty-six are tournament, match or recorded casual games with a provenance no worse than any other historical record. That is not the same as certainty: a game score is a document, and documents are copied, corrected and improved by their editors. What this book can promise is that it reproduces the document faithfully, which is the next section.

What the board checks

The source of a game is one file of plain text. The build reads it, sets up a board, and plays the game move by move. This catches the error that matters most and is hardest to see by eye: a move copied wrongly off a web page. A move the rules do not allow stops the build and prints the game, the move number, the move and the position it was tried in.

What is published is then what the board produced, not what was typed. If a source writes Qd4 where the position makes it a check, the book prints Qd4+, because the board says so.

Three further checks run on every build:

  • The result must match the final position. A game that ends in checkmate cannot be published as a draw, and a mate cannot be scored for the side mated. The last move must carry # exactly when it is mate.
  • The published PGN must replay to the same game. The PGN written for each chapter is read back with a parser that had nothing to do with writing it, and the moves it yields are compared with the record, ply by ply.
  • Nothing may be published twice, by title or by move list, and the chapters must be in chronological order.

make check runs all of them. It is not a guarantee that a game is the game that was played in 1851 – no amount of local checking can establish that – but it does guarantee that what is printed is a legal game of chess that matches its own result, its own PGN and its own source record.

Dates, and what precision means

A date is published to the precision that is known. Where the day is recorded, the day is printed; where only the year survives, only the year is printed, and the build will not silently turn 1623 into the first of January. Where a date is a tradition rather than a record, it is printed with “c.” in front of it, and the chapter says what the tradition is worth. Légal’s Mate is the clearest case: it is always dated 1750, and the research cited in its chapter puts it nearer 1787.

The notes

The notes are written for this book unless a note names whose judgement it is reporting. A note that carries a name is reporting that person’s assessment, not quoting them at length: Tartakower on Rotlewi’s tenth move, Chernev on Rubinstein’s rook, Nimzowitsch on his own game, Kasparov on Deep Blue. The source each was read in is listed with the game.

Where a game is famous for something that happened beside the board – the gold coins thrown at Marshall, von Bardeleben walking out of the hall, Spassky applauding Fischer – the chapter reports the story and reports what is known about whether it happened. Marshall’s shower of gold is the best example: he insisted on it, his wife denied it, and others said the coins were people settling bets.

What is reproduced, and on what footing

The moves are facts about events and are reproduced as such; a game score is a record of what was played, not an authored work. The writing around them is a different matter, and is handled in three ways.

Most notes were written for this book. Where a note reports somebody else’s assessment it carries their name, and where it uses their words it quotes them and keeps them short. There are eight such quotations in two hundred and thirty-five notes. Four are Nimzowitsch’s own notes to his own game, published in 1925 and long out of copyright, as are Steinitz on the Evergreen Game and the nineteenth-century assessments of the Opera Game. The remainder – Chernev, Tartakower, Hesse, Kasparov on Deep Blue – are brief attributed quotations from works still in copyright, used to say what their authors thought of a move.

The facts in the chapters – dates, places, rounds, what happened beside the board – are drawn from the sources listed with each game, Wikipedia among them, and facts carry no copyright. Each chapter names where its facts came from.

The book itself is software as much as it is writing: the board you play the games on is lichess-pgn-viewer, which is published under the GPL, and a page that embeds it is a combined work that can only be distributed under the same terms. So this book is GPL-3.0-or-later too, and the footer of every page links to the source it was built from, which is what that licence asks of a website.

What is not checked

An engine is not run over the games, and no move is called good or bad on an engine’s authority. Where a chapter calls a move a mistake, it is because a source that annotated the game called it one, and that source is listed. Marks like !! and ? are the annotators’ and are kept out of the notation for that reason.
